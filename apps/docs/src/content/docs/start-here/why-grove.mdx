Grove exists for teams who want **the source of truth to be a human-readable file in their repo** and want a static, deploy-anywhere output for it.

If your content already lives in files that your contributors are comfortable editing (YAML, Markdown, taxonomy files, JSON), Grove turns that content into a website plus a set of derived surfaces — without changing the way you write.

<Aside type="caution" title="When Grove isn't the right tool">
If your content lives in a database, a CMS, or a vendor service today, Grove is probably not the right tool yet. A database-driven system will keep performing better for problems that are themselves database-shaped.
</Aside>

## What Grove is — and what it isn't

<CardGrid stagger>
  <Card title="What Grove is">
    - <Badge text="+" variant="success" /> A **static-site-first framework** with built-in sync, classification, and review tools.
    - <Badge text="+" variant="success" /> A way to keep a single source of truth in files and let the rest follow.
    - <Badge text="+" variant="success" /> A publisher for both human-facing pages and machine-readable surfaces: `sitemap.xml`, `llms.txt`, `llms-full.txt`, JSON-LD, OG cards, JSON datasets.
    - <Badge text="+" variant="success" /> A way to keep external facts (stars, forks, archive status, contributors) current without manual effort.
  </Card>
  <Card title="What Grove isn't">
    - <Badge text="-" variant="danger" /> A **database**. There is no runtime; the build is fully static.
    - <Badge text="-" variant="danger" /> A **CMS**. Editors don't log in. Files are the editor.
    - <Badge text="-" variant="danger" /> A **directory starter**. A directory is one use case — not the boundary.
    - <Badge text="-" variant="danger" /> A **community platform**. Grove gives no chat, no auth, no reactions. Use the file system.
    - <Badge text="-" variant="danger" /> A **content-first renderer** you can fork and theme forever. Grove's presentations are stable and known; if you want unbounded design freedom, an empty Astro starter is a better choice.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## When Grove is the right call

<Aside type="tip">
Grove pays off when **the same knowledge needs to be authoritative everywhere at once**:

- You maintain a directory of OSS tools, agents, or libraries that dozens of contributors read.
- You publish a community resource hub that needs both a website and machine-readable feeds (RSS, JSON, `llms.txt`) for AI assistants.
- You want curatorial judgment (visibility, sort priority, "why this is listed") expressed in files, not in a moderation backend.
- You want a maintenance loop — refresh facts on a schedule, surface stale records, keep the dataset clean — without writing a cron job.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="When Grove isn't the right call">
Grove is **not** the right call if your content needs:

- Real-time editing in a browser.
- Approvals, scheduled publishing, or multi-stage workflows.
- Per-user personalization or paywall logic.
</Aside>

## A directory is one shape, not the definition

The reference space that ships with Grove — the one `grove init` copies — is a
project directory, and most of the examples in these docs follow it. That is a
starting point, not a boundary. Research collections, technology ecosystems,
editorial knowledge bases, and internal knowledge hubs are the same machinery
pointed at different content.

<Aside type="note" title="About this site">
These docs are **not** themselves a Grove space — they are a Starlight site that
borrows Grove's theme. The working example lives in the repository at
[`apps/example/`](https://github.com/tortuvshin/grove/tree/main/apps/example):
records, taxonomy, config, and the generated outputs, all in one place.
</Aside>
